Reflective Commentary

At the beginning of this course, I was interested in time series modelling but unsure how to put it into practice. I could understand individual concepts theoretically, but connecting them to examples and implementing them was more challenging. Through the tutorials and projects, I developed a better understanding of how the methods build on one another. My biggest achievement was becoming more confident with the entire modelling process, from exploring data and fitting models to interpreting output and deciding what to do next.

One of my earliest challenges was understanding the sequence of decisions involved in a time series analysis. In my first project, on forecasting influenza, I struggled to see how plotting the data, STL decomposition, assessing stationarity, differencing, examining the ACF and PACF, and choosing a model fitted together. I understood these more easily as separate topics than as parts of one analysis. Working through the textbook and asking how each section connected to the next helped me move beyond following a procedure towards understanding why each step was useful.

The third assignment helped reinforce these connections. It involved explanatory variables alongside ARMA modelling, and recognising the ARMA component helped me connect the work to my first project. The crime project is one of the projects I am most proud of because it allowed me to bring regression and time series modelling together. I began to see that the course was giving me tools that could be combined, rather than a collection of unrelated methods. Seeing these connections was one of the most rewarding surprises of the portfolio.

Exponential smoothing was the section I found easiest to understand. Separating a series into level, trend and seasonality gave me an intuitive way to think about forecasting. In my first project, exponential smoothing performed better than the ARIMA approach I considered. Initially, I associated this with exponential smoothing requiring fewer assumptions. My understanding is now more careful: both approaches have assumptions, and better performance in one example does not establish that one method is always preferable. The comparison showed me the importance of considering how well a method suits the data and assessing its actual forecasting performance. Exponential smoothing also opened the door to my interest in state space models.

The structural breaks tutorial changed how I thought about using historical data. Recent disruptions, including the pandemic, showed me that relationships observed in the past may change. Exploring Japan tourist arrivals helped me understand why using more historical data does not necessarily improve forecasts when the underlying pattern has changed. I became more interested in investigating the context of the data. When I noticed a dip or an unusual movement, I started asking what had happened during that period and looking for information that might explain it. I also recognise that a plausible explanation needs to be treated carefully rather than assumed to prove the cause of a change.

Although I found structural breaks interesting, translating them into code was difficult. The tutorial helped by breaking the model into understandable parts: the pattern before the disruption, an indicator for an immediate change, and a ramp for a change in trend. Some examples also included a dampening effect. This helped me see how different terms could represent different features of a disruption. However, much of the tutorial relied on visual identification. I would like to understand more formal statistical methods for detecting breaks and assessing the uncertainty around them.

My volatility project was another important point in my learning. I initially struggled with the theory of ARCH and GARCH models, particularly conditional variance. Going through proofs step by step, answering questions and applying the models to JSE Top 40 returns helped consolidate my understanding. Conditional variance became more meaningful when I connected it to the variability of returns given past information, rather than thinking of it as the change in the return itself.

Volatility clustering also became clearer through the project. Large absolute returns tended to occur together, while smaller absolute returns occurred together during quieter periods. I could then connect this pattern to how GARCH models use past squared innovations and past conditional variances to model current conditional variance. This helped me distinguish between modelling the conditional mean and modelling the uncertainty around it. A model can provide useful information about future volatility and the persistence of shocks, even when the direction of future returns remains difficult to predict. Working through the theory and then observing the pattern in real data gave me a deeper understanding than either activity alone.

My fourth project, exploring the Kalman filter, is also one I am proud of. Changing the scenarios allowed me to investigate its strengths and weaknesses rather than only applying it once. My favourite scenarios involved misspecified uncertainty and structural change because these felt relevant to the difficulties encountered with real data. In the structural-change scenario, it was interesting to see the filter adapt and recover. However, the uncertainty experiments showed me that this responsiveness depends strongly on the assumptions supplied to the filter.

In particular, increasing the assumed process uncertainty, \(Q\), made the filter more responsive to observations. Underestimating \(Q\) could produce too much smoothing and a slower response to changes, while overestimating it could make the estimates follow noise too closely. The measurement uncertainty, \(R\), affected how much the filter trusted the observations. Assuming that observations were more reliable than they actually were caused the filter to place too much weight on them. These experiments taught me that flexibility alone does not guarantee accurate estimates. The balance between trusting the model and trusting the observations needs to be considered carefully.

Across the portfolio, implementing methods from first principles was especially valuable because it tested my intuition. Writing code forced me to think about what each part of a method was doing. Explaining the results in my own words then helped me identify gaps in my understanding. I now feel more confident carrying out the coding procedure for a time series project and interpreting how the output should influence the next modelling decision.

AI supported several parts of this learning process. I used it to help develop tutorials on spectral analysis and structural breaks, assemble theoretical ARCH/GARCH questions, and generate the initial idea for my fourth project. I also used it for code explanations, interactive HTML learning materials, blank tutorial templates, formatting and grammar. To consolidate my understanding, I would write a paragraph explaining what I thought the code or concept meant and use follow-up questions to test that understanding.

However, I also learnt to be more selective about AI suggestions. Some tutorial questions were unclear or unsuitable. In one volatility tutorial, I checked the textbook and replaced a suggested question with a more appropriate proof, while leaving other questions out completely. This reinforced that I remained responsible for checking the material and understanding anything I included.

Some AI-assisted project ideas also led me away from my learning aims. I initially considered Japan visitor arrivals for my first project, but the pandemic disruption and subsequent regime change made the scope more complicated than I had expected. Feedback from my lecturer helped me understand the issue, but the analysis no longer aligned with what I wanted that project to achieve. I also spent over a week exploring a volatility Shiny app, including consulting a textbook, before recognising that building the interface was taking attention away from volatility modelling. Changing direction helped me focus on work from which I felt I could learn more. This taught me to judge project ideas by their learning value and manageable scope.

The portfolio also made me more aware of the importance of reproducibility and transparent documentation. Being able to run code is only part of communicating an analysis; the data sources, modelling choices and steps leading to the results also need to be clear. My AI log is an area I would improve. I sometimes used one conversation for several different tasks, which made it difficult to link specific assistance to individual pieces of work. In future, I would keep separate conversations for distinct tasks and record substantive AI use as I worked, including what I checked or changed myself.

I would also start assembling the portfolio earlier, alongside writing the journal entries. Bringing everything together showed me the value of organising the evidence of learning throughout the course. My perfectionism sometimes made it difficult to limit the scope of a task, particularly when I wanted to understand every detail before moving on. In future, I would set clearer aims and distinguish what is necessary for the current project from questions that could become later investigations.

There are still areas in which I need more practice. Hidden Markov models remain challenging, especially connecting the theory to practical applications. The additional spectral analysis tutorial improved my understanding, but I am still less confident recognising when the method would be useful. I would also like to explore Bayesian approaches more deeply. The Kalman filter introduced me to ideas about updating knowledge as observations arrive, and I would like to develop that understanding further. Another priority is investigating how structural breaks can be incorporated into volatility and state space modelling.

At the beginning of the course, I was interested in time series modelling but unsure how to put it into practice. I now feel that I have a good foundation for exploring more complex models. The portfolio helped me see how theory, computation and interpretation support one another, and how different methods can be connected through a common modelling problem. I am more confident working with real-world data and excited about the projects I will undertake in the future.
